## Why I'm Writing This

Every AI product launch seems to chase the same thing: the most impressive possible demo. A fully autonomous agent that books your entire trip. A chatbot that "does anything." A magic button that rewrites your whole workflow. These make for great launch videos. But when I look at which AI features people actually keep using week after week, it's almost never the flashy ones. It's the small, quiet, slightly boring ones: autocomplete, summarize this email, suggest a subject line, clean up this paragraph.

That pattern is consistent enough that I think it's worth taking seriously.

## What I Mean by "Small and Boring"

A small, boring AI feature usually has a few things in common:

- **It does one narrow thing.** Not "help with anything," but "summarize this thread" or "fix the grammar in this sentence."
- **It lives inside a workflow the user already has.** No new app to open, no new habit to form. It shows up where the work is already happening.
- **Its output is easy to check.** You can see the result in two seconds and decide whether to accept it.
- **A wrong answer is cheap.** If the suggestion is bad, you ignore it and lose a few seconds, not an afternoon.

Nothing about this makes for a viral demo. That's exactly the point.

## Why the Flashy Features Struggle

Flashy AI features tend to share the opposite traits, and each one creates real friction in practice:

- **They're broad, so they fail in unpredictable ways.** A "do anything" assistant has an enormous surface area for mistakes, and users can't build a reliable sense of when to trust it.
- **They demand a behavior change.** Asking users to open a new interface, learn a new way of phrasing requests, or hand over a whole task requires effort before any value shows up.
- **Their errors are expensive.** If an agent takes ten steps and gets step four wrong, the user has to untangle everything that followed. Trust drops fast after one bad experience like that.
- **They're hard to evaluate.** When the output is long, complex, or action-based, checking it takes as much effort as doing the task yourself, which cancels out the benefit.
- **Demos hide the failure rate.** A flashy feature that works 70% of the time looks perfect in a curated demo and frustrating in daily use.

## Why Small Features Compound

Small features win because of how trust and habit actually form:

- **Reliability builds trust quickly.** A feature that's right 95% of the time on a narrow task earns confidence fast, while a broad feature that's right 70% of the time keeps users on guard forever.
- **Frequent use creates habit.** A small feature that saves ten seconds, a hundred times a week, becomes something people can't imagine working without. A big feature used twice a month never becomes part of anyone's routine.
- **Low stakes encourage experimentation.** When a bad suggestion costs almost nothing, people try the feature more often and discover more uses for it.
- **They're easier to improve.** A narrow feature has clear success criteria, so teams can measure it, test prompt changes, and make it better steadily. A vague, sprawling feature is hard to even define as "working."

## A Simple Comparison

Consider two features in an email app:

**Flashy:** "AI manages your inbox autonomously, replies to messages on your behalf, and schedules your week."

**Boring:** "AI suggests a one-line summary at the top of long email threads."

The first sounds revolutionary, but it needs deep trust, touches high-stakes actions, and fails in ways that are embarrassing or costly. The second is almost forgettable, but it's right nearly every time, saves real time daily, and a mistake is harmless because the full email is one tap away. After a month, most people would have noticed and kept using the second one. Many would have turned the first one off.

## This Connects to Something I Said Earlier

In an earlier post on whether every app needs a built-in AI agent, I argued that most apps would end up with AI-assisted features rather than full autonomous agents. This is the same idea seen from the product side: the features that survive contact with real users tend to be narrow, assistive, and low-risk, not sweeping and autonomous. The pattern holds for the same underlying reason, which is that usefulness depends on reliability and fit, not on how impressive the capability sounds.

## Where Flashy Features Do Make Sense

To be fair, flashy isn't always wrong:

- **Flashy features can draw attention and attract first-time users**, even if retention comes from the quieter features underneath.
- **Some problems genuinely need ambitious solutions.** Complex research, multi-step coding tasks, and long workflows can justify a more capable, agentic approach, as long as the reliability is actually there.
- **Today's flashy feature becomes tomorrow's boring one.** Autocomplete and spell-check once felt futuristic. Features that earn trust gradually become invisible infrastructure.

The lesson isn't "never be ambitious." It's that ambition needs to be matched by reliability, and that the boring version of an idea often reaches that bar first.

## What This Means If You're Building Something

If you're deciding which AI feature to build next, a few questions help:

- **Can a user verify the output in a few seconds?** If not, the feature needs a much higher accuracy bar before it's worth shipping.
- **What does a wrong answer cost?** The cheaper the mistake, the safer it is to ship early and learn from real usage.
- **Does it fit into something the user already does?** Features that meet people inside existing workflows get used. Features that require a new habit often don't.
- **Could you ship a narrower version first?** Starting with the smallest useful slice, then expanding as trust grows, is usually more successful than launching the grand vision all at once.

## Closing Thought

The AI features that quietly become part of people's daily routine are rarely the ones that made the best launch demo. They're the small ones that work almost every time, cost almost nothing when they don't, and show up exactly where the work already happens. Boring turns out to be a feature, not a flaw: it usually just means the thing is reliable enough to stop thinking about, which is the real goal of most software anyway.
